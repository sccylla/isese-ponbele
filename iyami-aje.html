ÌYÁMI ÀJẸ́

A careful cultural documentary on the Yoruba ideas often gathered under the names Àjẹ́, Àwọn Ìyá (“the Mothers”), Ìyámi (“my mothers”) and, in some traditions, Ìyámi Òṣòròngà. The subject is powerful, sensitive and frequently mistranslated. This page separates documented cultural ideas from sensational “witchcraft” stereotypes.

Important distinction

Ìyámi Àjẹ́ should not be presented as one simple, universally defined Òrìṣà with a fixed icon, colour or shrine. In scholarly and practitioner accounts, Àjẹ́ can refer to an extraordinary spiritual power and to those who possess or exercise that power; Àwọn Ìyá names the powerful “Mothers.” Meanings vary by region, lineage and context.

1. Language, names and translation

The words used around this subject matter carry more meaning than a single English translation can hold. Ìyá means mother; Ìyámi means “my mother” or “my mothers” depending on context; Àwọn Ìyá means “the Mothers.” The word Àjẹ́ is especially difficult because it can refer to a spiritual capacity, a person associated with that capacity, or an entire field of moral and cosmological ideas.

English translations have often used the word “witch,” especially in colonial, missionary and early anthropological writing. That translation captures only the fearful or destructive side of the concept and can erase the generative, protective, judicial and balancing dimensions that many Yoruba accounts also emphasize. Teresa N. Washington’s work argues strongly that Àjẹ́ is a central form of female spiritual power that has often been misunderstood when filtered through European categories.

Translation note: this documentary uses “Mothers” for Àwọn Ìyá and leaves Àjẹ́ untranslated whenever possible. That keeps the Yoruba concept visible instead of forcing it into one English category.

2. What does Àjẹ́ mean?

Across Yoruba thought, Àjẹ́ is connected with extraordinary efficacy: the ability to affect life, social order, prosperity, fertility, protection, judgment and misfortune. The same power can be described as beneficial, dangerous or corrective depending on the moral relationship between people and the community.

That ambiguity is important. Yoruba religious categories frequently resist simple “good versus evil” divisions. Power can create and destroy; authority can protect and sanction. The language around Àjẹ́ often expresses this double capacity. To describe the Mothers only as harmful beings therefore misses the broader cultural logic in which power must be respected because it is capable of sustaining or destabilizing social life.

In Washington’s interpretation, Àjẹ́ is deeply tied to creativity, birth, justice and the capacity of women to shape social existence. Older ethnographic descriptions often focused much more heavily on fear. Reading sources from different periods makes it clear that there is not one neutral translation: every author brings assumptions about gender, religion and power.

3. Àwọn Ìyá — “the Mothers”

“The Mothers” is a respectful collective expression. In many narratives the category includes powerful elder women, female ancestors and spiritual powers associated with motherhood, seniority and social authority. Their importance is not simply biological motherhood; age, experience, generative capacity and the ability to bless or sanction are central themes.

Gèlèdé traditions are one of the clearest public cultural expressions of this respect. UNESCO describes Gèlèdé as a ceremony of Yoruba-Nago communities in Benin, Nigeria and Togo that honors the primordial mother Ìyá Nlá and the role of women in social organization and development. The ceremony’s songs, masks, dance and satire publicly affirm that the Mothers must be acknowledged.

The central idea is not that women are secretly dangerous by nature; it is that motherhood, seniority and female spiritual authority are powers that society cannot safely ignore.

4. Birds and the symbolism of flight

Bird imagery appears repeatedly in accounts of the Mothers. UNESCO’s description of Gèlèdé identifies the bird as a messenger of “the Mothers.” Older ethnographic literature also links Àjẹ́ with night flight and transformation into birds, though those texts often frame the subject in strongly fearful language.

Why birds? In symbolic terms, flight suggests mobility between ordinary and extraordinary space, the ability to see from above, rapid movement and access to hidden places. Birds also appear prominently on Gèlèdé masks and other Yoruba works of art. These images should be understood symbolically and historically rather than treated as literal proof of supernatural claims.

5. Gèlèdé: public art for the Mothers

Gèlèdé is among the most important bodies of evidence for understanding public Yoruba attitudes toward female spiritual power. It is practiced especially among western Yoruba and Yoruba-Nago communities. The performances combine carved headpieces, elaborate costumes, drumming, singing, dance, satire and social commentary.

UNESCO inscribed the Oral Heritage of Gèlèdé on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. Its documentation explains that the performances honor Ìyá Nlá and women’s social roles. Animal figures—including birds—appear in the sculptural language of the masks. Gèlèdé therefore provides a public, artistic vocabulary for themes that may otherwise be treated cautiously in everyday speech.

Henry John Drewal and Margaret Thompson Drewal’s landmark study Gèlèdé: Art and Female Power among the Yoruba examines the masquerade as a total performance system rather than just a set of carved objects. Their work connects sculpture, song, dance, gender and social regulation, and remains one of the most influential studies of the subject.

6. Gender, seniority and authority

Many sources emphasize that Àjẹ́ is strongly associated with women, particularly mature and elder women. That association does not mean every woman is treated as possessing the same spiritual status, nor does it mean men are completely absent from the concept. Some accounts allow for men to possess Àjẹ́, while still identifying women as its primary owners or controllers.

Oyeronke Olajubu’s scholarship is useful here because it places women’s sacred roles inside a wider Yoruba system of gender relations rather than isolating them as “witchcraft.” Her work shows that women occupy central ritual, economic and social positions and that religious power is negotiated through complementary and sometimes contested relationships.

The concept of seniority is equally important. Yoruba social thought often gives weight to age, lineage position and accumulated experience. “Mother” can therefore signal authority and social depth, not merely parenthood.

7. Is Ìyámi Àjẹ́ an Òrìṣà?

There is no single answer accepted by every Yoruba community or lineage. In many scholarly treatments, Àjẹ́ is better understood as a form of power and “the Mothers” as a collective category rather than as one individually named Òrìṣà. Practitioner traditions may use different theological language.

For that reason, Isese Ponbele keeps the Òrìṣà documentary and this Ìyámi Àjẹ́ documentary connected but distinct. The relationship between the Mothers, Òrìṣà, ancestors, human elders and social institutions is complex and should not be flattened into a modern chart.

8. Common misconceptions

“Àjẹ́ simply means evil witchcraft.”

This is too narrow. Harm and fear appear in many accounts, but so do creativity, fertility, justice, protection, correction and social balance. Translation history matters.

“Every Yoruba person believes exactly the same thing.”

Yoruba religion has strong local traditions. Ketu, Ẹ̀gbá, Ọ̀yọ́, Ilé-Ifẹ̀, Ìjẹ̀ṣà, Ondo, Ekiti and diaspora communities may preserve different terminology and emphases.

“Gèlèdé is a festival of witches.”

That phrasing is reductive. UNESCO presents Gèlèdé as an oral heritage honoring Ìyá Nlá and women’s roles through masks, dance, music and social commentary.

“There is one official portrait of Ìyámi.”

There is no single canonical physical image. The strongest documented visual traditions are symbolic: birds, female figures, elderhood, masks, costume and the artistic language of Gèlèdé.

9. Contemporary relevance

Today the language of Àjẹ́ appears in scholarship, popular spirituality, literature, diaspora traditions and online discussions. This wider visibility has created both new appreciation and new distortions. Some modern presentations romanticize Àjẹ́ as a universal “divine feminine”; others sensationalize it as secret occult power. Both can erase the specific Yoruba historical and linguistic context.

A responsible modern approach keeps three things in view: language, local variation and source criticism. Oral knowledge and practitioner testimony are essential, but so are careful historical records, museum collections and scholarship. Sensitive ritual information also has its own boundaries; a public documentary does not need to expose restricted practices in order to take the tradition seriously.

10. Images, masks and material culture

Because Ìyámi Àjẹ́ does not have one fixed portrait, this documentary uses documented Gèlèdé objects as its visual anchor. These works publicly materialize ideas about the Mothers, female seniority, satire and social power.
